Field Notes: 01 · Introduction to Prompt Engineering

Big Idea: A language model produces a continuation of the information it receives. Prompt engineering is the practice of making that continuation useful, relevant, and reliable.

The Central Question

Imagine giving a language model the words: “The most important discovery of the experiment was…”. Many continuations are possible. The model could finish a scientific report, invent a dramatic story, or produce a short summary. The words surrounding the request determine which kind of continuation is likely.

That is the starting point of prompt engineering: designing the instructions, examples, and context so that the model's output serves a particular purpose. It is not simply the art of asking a clever question. In an application, prompt engineering also means deciding what information to retrieve, how to structure a conversation, and what to do with the result.

1. How Did We Get Here?

The path from early language models to conversational assistants can be understood as a sequence of solutions to information-processing problems:

Era Key development What changed?
1940s onward Statistical language models and Markov approaches Predict text from a limited preceding context.
2014 Sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) models Translate or transform one sequence into another with an encoder and decoder.
2015 Attention in neural sequence models Allow a decoder to access relevant portions of the input rather than relying on one compressed vector.
2017 The Transformer architecture Use attention as the central mechanism for representing relationships within a sequence.
2018 GPT: Generative Pre-trained Transformer Pretrain a transformer-based text generator, then adapt it to useful tasks.
GPT-2 → GPT-3 → GPT-4 Increasingly capable language models Prompting becomes an important way to perform tasks without training a new model for each one.

Remember: The transformation was not from “predicting words” to an entirely different principle. It was from simple predictions with limited context to powerful context-sensitive prediction.

2. The Original Challenge: A Sentence is Not a Single Vector

An early seq2seq translation model had two main components: an encoder that compressed the source sentence into a single thought vector, and a decoder that generated the translated tokens. This created an information bottleneck where details were lost on long sequences.

Without attention With attention
One summary must carry most input information. The decoder can consult a collection of source representations.
Details can disappear in long sequences. Different source words can become important at different output steps.
A narrow information bottleneck. More flexible access to context.

3. The Three Limitations Worth Remembering

  • A language model is not automatically a search engine: It can produce plausible text without having retrieved or verified a source.
  • Context is finite: A model processes only the content available to it within its supported context limit.
  • Fluent does not mean correct: A well-written explanation can still contain factual or logical errors. Verification matters most when errors have consequences.

The Training Pipeline: SFT → Feedback → RLHF

  • Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): Human demonstrations show what good assistant replies look like.
  • Human Preference Comparisons: Evaluators rank multiple candidate responses.
  • Reward Modeling: A neural reward model learns to predict human preference scores.
  • Reinforcement Learning (PPO): Optimizes policy behavior against the reward signal while mitigating reward hacking.

The Four Requirements of an Effective Prompt

  1. A familiar format: Structure like a recognizable document or dialogue (The Little Red Riding Hood principle).
  2. Enough relevant information: Supply what is necessary; omit clutter.
  3. A clear direction: Tell the model what to accomplish and how to begin.
  4. A controlled stopping point: Define the output boundary explicitly.

Feedforward Pipeline: 4 Stages

Stage What happens? Example
1. Context retrieval Collect useful information. Read the relevant support article.
2. Snippetization Split long material into manageable excerpts. Extract the paragraph about fan noise.
3. Prioritization Rank pieces by usefulness and relevance. Device-specific instructions before generic tips.
4. Prompt assembly Combine task, constraints, and context. Build the final troubleshooting request.
